Ping Pong Recirculation System

An autonomous launch-and-recover machine: fire six ping pong balls through a 6″ × 6″ hoop, catch them, reload them, and repeat for 60 seconds with no human touch after one button press.

110passes in 60 s
1stplace in class

01

The challenge

Most passes in 60 seconds wins

Launch balls through a hoop, recirculate them without human help, and count valid passes. The ranking came down to one thing: how many balls made it through.

  • Hoop opening 6″ × 6″, placed at least 12″ out and 12″ up from the launch point
  • Only 6 balls; no tubes or funnels guiding balls at the hoop
  • Launcher and hoop can’t be rigidly connected; everything fits one desk

  • Worked on the return loop that gets caught balls back into the launcher

04

Approach

What we did differently: launch, recover, reload, repeat

Other teams returned their balls through enclosed tubes. We let ours fly.

Two-wheel flywheel launcher

Two 3D-printed wheels with a grip surface pinch each ball from both sides for fast, repeatable launches, with a speed of approximately 5 m/s, measured by our camera system.

Open bounce-back return

A ball goes through the hoop, rebounds off a curved backboard and drops into a net, and a funnel and small feed motor send it back to the wheels. That removed the slowest part of recirculation.

Camera counting, not beam-break

Our first hoop used IR beams, but it missed very fast balls and would also have counted balls on their way back. A thin hoop with a camera connected to a teammate’s laptop solved both, and the laptop sent the launcher its start and stop signals.

05

Mechanical design

The launcher, iteration by iteration

The wheels are about 2 inches in diameter on DC motors. The mount and funnel were designed as one system, so changing the funnel meant revising the mount, which led to many iterations.

The Angry Birds theme

The hoop is a laser-cut cardboard Angry Birds tower with green pig ping pong balls and laser-engraved TNT crates, and red birds sit on the launcher mounts.

06

Electronics

Prototyping the sensing and drive circuits

Before the team switched to camera counting, I built an 8-sensor IR hoop that measured ball speed in m/s to within ±5%. To check it, we dropped balls from rest 1 ft above the horizontal hoop: free fall predicts about 2.4 m/s (v = √(2gh)).

Speed from two planes

Two planes of four IR break beams. The Arduino timestamped each plane in microseconds and divided the distance between them by the time.

Interrupts for fast balls

One beam per plane used a hardware interrupt to timestamp fast balls instantly, with the others polled as backup. A pass counted only if the entrance plane tripped first, and a 200 ms lockout prevented double counts.

Launcher start-up

I wired the launcher so one button spins up both flywheels through an L298N driver. A MOSFET-switched funnel motor starts 2.5 seconds later, so the wheels are at speed when the first ball arrives.

07

Problems solved

What broke, and what fixed it

Motors shook the mounts apart

Sustained vibration broke the printed motor mounts. I added washers for smoother spinning and kept iterating the mount until it survived full runs.

The wheel grip kept failing

Rubber bands on the flywheels fell off or snapped after repeated use at high speeds. A high-grip textured sticker material, superglued on, stayed put.

Balls escaped the net

Fast balls bounced out of the first fabric catcher. We switched to the net and added reinforcing panels where testing showed balls escaping. A slightly loose net absorbs the energy of returning balls, so we never had to slow the launcher to keep up.

Jams in the funnel

Too many balls were entering the funnel at once. A small feed motor helped early on, and once launches were fast enough, balls never queued up.

08

Next steps

What I learned, and what I’d change in version 2

  • Failures drove the design. Each fix above came from watching real runs.
  • Measuring fast events is hard. In the official competition run, our camera caught at most 86 of the 110 passes that we and the professors counted live.
  • Speed has a cost. Our open return was faster but riskier, so the net had to be built to handle what went wrong.

Version 2: what I’d design in from the start

Enclosure and wiring, planned together

I’d plan the wiring and the motor enclosure together from the start, so everything routes in cleanly and the launch angle is easy to adjust. I’d also 3D print the enclosure or use something sturdier than our laser-cut cardboard.

A camera built into the system

Our counting camera looked down from above, which was awkward to set up. I’d build it in low, on the floor under the hoop, positioned to catch every ball.
