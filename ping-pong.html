Ping Pong Recirculation System

An autonomous launch-and-recover machine: fire six ping pong balls through a 6″ × 6″ hoop, catch them, reload them, and repeat for 60 seconds with no human touch after one button press.

My role

The launcher and the return loop

  • Designed and 3D-printed the launcher mounts and funnel, iterating through many revisions
  • Iterated the catch net and its reinforcement through repeated testing
  • Worked on the return loop that gets caught balls back into the launcher
  • Wrote the launcher control code (L298N flywheel motors, MOSFET-driven funnel motor, one-button start) on an Arduino; the team moved it to an ESP32 at the end, where a teammate extended it
  • Early in the project, prototyped the IR hoop sensing before the team switched to a camera

The challenge

Most passes in 60 seconds wins

The brief: launch ping pong balls through a hoop, recirculate them without human help, and count valid passes, all in 60 seconds from a single button press. The ranking came down to one thing: how many balls made it through the hoop.

  • Hoop opening 6″ × 6″, placed 12″ out and 12″ up from the launch point
  • Only 6 balls; no tubes or funnels guiding balls at the hoop
  • Launcher and hoop can’t be rigidly connected; everything fits one desk

The result

110

passes in the official 60-second run, first place in the class competition.

What we did differently

Launch, recover, reload, repeat

Many teams returned their balls through enclosed tubes. We let ours fly: a ball goes through the hoop, rebounds off a curved backboard, drops into a net, and a funnel feeds it straight back to the wheels. The open path risks stray balls, but it recirculates much faster.

Two-wheel flywheel launcher

Two 3D-printed wheels with a grip surface pinch each ball from both sides, giving fast, repeatable launches without a single-wheel or air-powered setup.

Open bounce-back return

Instead of a closed return tube, the ball rebounds off a curved backboard into a net. That removed the slowest part of recirculation.

Camera counting, not beam-break

Our first hoop counted passes with IR beams, but it missed very fast balls, and a thick sensor hoop would also have counted balls on their way back. A thin decorative hoop with camera counting solved both.

Catch net with slack

A slightly loose net absorbs the energy of returning balls so they stay put, which meant we never had to slow the launcher to keep up.

How it works

Launch, bounce, catch, feed

  1. Launch. Two spinning wheels grip each ball and fire it through the hoop.
  2. Bounce. The ball rebounds off a curved backboard.
  3. Catch. A net with slack absorbs its energy and holds it.
  4. Feed. A funnel and a small feed motor drop balls back between the wheels.

A camera on a teammate’s laptop counted the passes and sent the start and stop signals to the launcher.

Mechanical design

The launcher, iteration by iteration

The wheels are about 2 inches in diameter on DC motors. The mount and funnel were designed as one system, so changing the funnel meant revising the mount, which led to many iterations.

Electronics

Prototyping the sensing and drive circuits

Before the team switched to camera counting, I built an 8-sensor IR hoop prototype that measured ball velocity to within ±5%. I also wired the launcher’s motor driver and funnel motor for the Arduino control code.

Problems I solved

What broke, and what fixed it

Motors shook the mounts apart

The DC motors were strong enough to break their printed mounts from sustained vibration. I added washers for smoother spinning and kept iterating the mount design until it survived full runs.

The wheel grip kept failing

Rubber bands on the flywheels fell off or snapped at speed. I switched to a high-grip textured sticker material and superglued it on so it stayed put at full speed.

Balls escaped the net

The first fabric catcher let fast balls bounce out. I switched to a net with slack to absorb their energy and added reinforcing panels in the spots that testing showed balls escaping from.

Jams in the funnel

I traced jams to too many balls entering the funnel at once. A small feed motor helped early on, and once launches were fast enough, balls never queued up in the first place.

The Angry Birds theme

Built to look like the game

The hoop is a laser-cut cardboard Angry Birds tower with green pig ping pong balls and laser-engraved TNT crates. It carries no electronics, since the camera does the counting; it’s there for the assignment and the theme. Red birds sit on the launcher mounts, and the backboard sits above the pigs.

Takeaways

What I took away

  • Failures drove the design. Broken mounts, slipping wheel grip and escaping balls each led to a specific fix, found by watching real runs.
  • Details matter at speed. At these motor speeds, vibration, grip and net slack decided whether the system worked.
  • Measuring fast events is hard. Our camera counter peaked at 86 in testing against 110 passes in the official run.
  • Speed over safety only works if you engineer for the failure modes.
